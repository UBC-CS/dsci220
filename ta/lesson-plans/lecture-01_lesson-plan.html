Sages & Tricksters

Lecture 1 lesson plan, DSCI 220, 2026W1

Instructor and TA material. Not rendered into the student site.

Setup

  • 15 envelopes, each with a printed image of its 6 cards on the outside and, inside, the 6 cards, the fact slip and a reference card.
  • Groups of 6, TAs filling gaps. ~72 enrolled, so expect about 12 groups.
  • Roles come from the colour of the card each player draws. Black = Sage (truth), red = Trickster (lies). This is the one thing the old materials never wrote down, and students cannot play without it.
  • The card image is public, so the Trickster count is public too — count the reds. Say this; without it the all-Sage and all-Trickster readings are indistinguishable.

Rules — as printed on the cards

  1. Sages answer truthfully. Tricksters always answer falsely.
  2. Two info rounds. Each round every player asks one yes/no question; everyone answers about their own card.
  3. Debate round. Compare answers and look for contradictions.
  4. Conclusion. Agree on everyone’s roles, then reveal.

The fact slips are decorative: the six cards are printed on the envelope, so anything true of them is already public and the fact adds no information. Harmless to leave in; nothing depends on them.

One defect. The to-do list offered Packet 12 a second fact, “all spade cards are even.” It is ill-defined — the only spade is Q♠ and a Queen is neither even nor odd. If that is the slip in the envelope, swap it for the kings-and-queens one.

Packets 11 and 14 have a single Trickster and 12 has five. All are playable; they just run fast or strange. Packet 12 is worth calling on in the debrief, since with five liars a majority vote gives that group confidently the wrong answer.

Driving the debrief

Two questions are dead, and both are worth letting them discover:

Question Result Why
“Is your card red?” everyone says no a black card isn’t red and says so; a red card is and denies it
“Is your card black?” everyone says yes the same collapse, inverted

With twelve questions available, burning one on these costs nothing and makes the point better than any warning would.

Then drive the discussion from the Lying is an operator slide. Reveal the right column one fragment at a time and let them supply the last step. The answer to reach: ask about a property whose value you already know for that person. That fixes the “has it” column, leaving two rows that differ in the output — so their answer reports the lie bit directly.

Either value works. Fix the column to 0 and a “yes” means lying; fix it to 1 and a “no” means lying. If a group offers one direction, ask for the other.

Known-false is much easier to find in practice: after a “yes” the surviving cards are (black with the property) together with (red without it), spanning both colours, so naming a suit or rank none of them has is easy while finding something they all share is rare.

Handoff

Two claims about the room, both established during the game:

  • “Priya is a Trickster” — settled by finding a contradiction. Certain.
  • “Most questions were about the same card” — settled by counting. Supported.

Both are arguments. This course is the first kind. Do not oversell the connection; it gets earned in lecture 3, when a boolean mask turns out to be a predicate evaluated over rows.
