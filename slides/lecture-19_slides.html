The Word “Set”

A set is an unordered collection of distinct items.

  • \(\{1, 2, 3\} = \{3, 1, 2\} = \{1, 2, 2, 3\}\).


A set is often defined by a rule, \(S = \{x : P(x)\}\). \[\color{#3d9970}{\textbf{green}} = \{\, x : x \in \{1, \dots, 31\} \text{ and } x \text{ has a } 1 \text{ in the } 8\text{s place} \,\}\]


The items in a set are its elements: \(x \in S\) exactly when \(P(x)\) is true.

 

\(24 \in \color{#3d9970}{\textbf{green}}\), because \(24 = (11000)_2\). \(\quad\) \(18 \notin \color{#3d9970}{\textbf{green}}\), because \(18 = (10010)_2\).

For green, P(x) is “x is in 1..31 and x has a 1 in the 8s place”. 24 = 16 + 8 = (11000)_2: a 1 in the 8s place, so 24 is on the green card. 5 = (101)_2: a 0 in the 8s place, so P(5) is false and 5 is not on it. The rule is a predicate (lecture 8), and in pandas a predicate is a mask. That’s the bridge for the rest of the hour.

Sets = Filters on Rows

 

Let \(U\) be all rows of a dataframe df.

  • A set \(A\subseteq U\) selects rows by a predicate \(P_A(x)\).
  • In code, a set is a Boolean mask: A_mask = P_A(row).

 

Key Ideas

  • Membership: \(x\in A \iff P_A(x)\iff\) mask on row \(x\) is True
  • Cardinality: \(|A| = \sum_{x\in U} \mathbf{1}_A(x)\iff\) A_mask.sum()
  • Subset: \(A\subseteq B \iff \forall x, P_A(x)\Rightarrow P_B(x)\iff\) ((A & ~B).sum()==0)

Say: “We’ll reason with three parallel languages: math sets, logical predicates, and dataframe masks. They’re equivalent.”

Math ⇄ Predicate ⇄ Dataframe

\(U\) is all the rows of df: the \(100\) students in our class.

Set Set-builder In pandas
\(C\) \(\{\,x : \mathrm{coffee}(x)\,\}\) df[df['coffee']]
\(M\) \(\{\,x : \mathrm{morning}(x)\,\}\) df[df['morning']]
\(C \cap M\) \(\{\,x : \mathrm{coffee}(x) \text{ and } \mathrm{morning}(x)\,\}\) df[df['coffee'] & df['morning']]

The set-builder drops “x in U”: once the universe is clear we don’t always write it (contrast the green card, which spelled out x in {1, …, 31}). Same class as the cards. C and M are the sets from Hold Up Your Card, and the third row is their intersection: the set C cap M, “and” in the set-builder, & in pandas. The predicate sits inside the braces in math and inside the brackets in pandas (df[‘coffee’] is the predicate as a True/False column); the rows that come out are the set. In the class: |C| = 56, |M| = 45, |C cap M| = 31.

Operations: Three Ways to Say the Same Thing

Let \(A,B\subseteq U\).

Operation Math (set) Predicate (logic) Dataframe (mask)
Union \(A\cup B\) \(\{x: P_A(x)\lor P_B(x)\}\) (A | B)
Intersection \(A\cap B\) \(\{x: P_A(x)\land P_B(x)\}\) (A & B)
Difference \(A-B\) \(\{x: P_A(x)\land \neg P_B(x)\}\) (A & ~B)
Complement \(A^c\) \(\{x: \neg P_A(x)\}\) ~A
Symm. diff. \(A\triangle B\) \(\{x:(P_A\lor P_B)\land \neg(P_A\land P_B)\}\) A ^ B

Precedence note: use &, |, ~ with parentheses: (A & B) | (~C).

Set Design

Get into groups of 4 with your cards.

  1. What is the fewest questions that give all 4 of you different answers? Which ones?
  2. Find a question all 4 of you answer the same way.
  3. What’s special about your group? Write a rule \(\{\, x : P(x) \,\}\) that includes all 4 of you and nobody else in the room.

Each question is a set; two people are told apart when they are not in exactly the same sets. One question splits a group into at most 2, so 4 people need at least 2 questions (2^2 = 4 answer pairs). A group holding a twin pair (Amara & Ravi, Leila & Hana, and others: 78 of the 100 share a card) can never be told apart – that is the next slide, for the whole class. 2. A shared answer puts the whole group inside one set: all in C, or all in C^c. Some groups share nothing – also worth saying out loud. 3. Start from the shared answers (an AND); check who else in the room fits by asking them to stand. If no AND works, an OR of the four exact descriptions always does – unless someone outside the group holds the same card as someone inside it. “Describe a group and nobody else” is a marketing segment, and it is the same question as the next slide. Chose a set of columns all at once, not ask-then-branch: no decision trees (DSCI 221).

Who Can Be Identified?

Six yes/no questions give \(2^6 = 64\) possible cards.

  • We have \(100\) students and only \(64\) possible cards. What does that guarantee?
  • In real data, a few “questions” with many possible answers are enough:
What identifies you Out of Identified
4 phone location pings (which tower, which hour) 1.5 million people 95%
4 credit-card purchases (which shop, which day) 1.1 million people 90%
about 8 movie ratings, with approximate dates Netflix subscribers 99%

Only 22 of the 100 are alone on their card; the class uses 54 of the 64 possible cards, and the biggest group shares one card four ways. Guaranteed: 100 students, 64 cards, so at least two share a card – in fact at most 64 can be alone, so at least 36 share (the pigeonhole principle; capacity from lecture 17). We come back to this dataset for the pigeonhole principle. Sources: de Montjoye et al., “Unique in the Crowd”, Scientific Reports 2013 (phone); de Montjoye et al., “Unique in the shopping mall”, Science 2015 (credit cards); Narayanan & Shmatikov 2008 (Netflix Prize). The classic is Sweeney (ZIP, birth date, sex) – left off the slide on purpose. The data-science response: k-anonymity – release data only if every row shares its profile with at least k - 1 others. Our class is not even 2-anonymous (22 students are unique). Why 4 pings beat 6 questions: a ping isn’t yes/no – it is one of thousands of towers times thousands of hours, millions of possible answers. Four of them give vastly more possible cards than people; we have 64 cards (6 bits) for 100 people. Fraction alone ~ e^(-n/m): e^(-100/64) ~ 21 students (actual 22). From subsets to dataframe masks

A Boolean mask over rows is an indicator vector \(\in\{0,1\}^{|U|}\).

  • Choosing rows is the same as selecting a subset of the universe \(U\).
  • \(|\)mask\(|\) (sum of Trues) = cardinality of that subset.

Union Example

Let
\(A=\{x\mid x.\mathrm{is\_iced}\}\) and
\(B=\{x\mid x.\mathrm{milk}\in\{\text{Oat, Almond}\}\}\).

  • Math: \(A\cup B\)
  • Predicate: \(\{\,x\in U \mid x.\mathrm{is\_iced}\ \lor\ x.\mathrm{milk}\in\{\text{Oat, Almond}\}\,\}\)
  • Mask: (df['is_iced']) | (df['milk'].str.strip().isin(['Oat','Almond']))
