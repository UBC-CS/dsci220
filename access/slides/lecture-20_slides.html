From subsets to dataframe masks

A Boolean mask over rows is an indicator vector \(\in\{0,1\}^{|U|}\).

  • Choosing rows is the same as selecting a subset of the universe \(U\).
  • \(|\)mask\(|\) (sum of Trues) = cardinality of that subset.

This names what The Class in Code just did: C is a column of 100 Trues and Falses, and |C| is the number of Trues. Friday’s power set picks this up: a mask over U is a |U|-bit string, so there are 2^|U| possible sets.

Who Are We?

Set Design

Get into groups of 4 with your cards.

  1. What is the fewest questions that give all 4 of you different answers? Which ones?
  2. Find a question all 4 of you answer the same way.
  3. What’s special about your group? Write a rule \(\{\, x : P(x) \,\}\) that includes all 4 of you and nobody else in the room.

Each question is a set; two people are told apart when they are not in exactly the same sets. One question splits a group into at most 2, so 4 people need at least 2 questions (2^2 = 4 answer pairs). A group holding a twin pair (Amara & Ravi, Leila & Hana, and others: 78 of the 100 share a card) can never be told apart – that is the next slide, for the whole class. 2. A shared answer puts the whole group inside one set: all in C, or all in C^c. Some groups share nothing – also worth saying out loud. 3. Start from the shared answers (an AND); check who else in the room fits by asking them to stand. If no AND works, an OR of the four exact descriptions always does – unless someone outside the group holds the same card as someone inside it. “Describe a group and nobody else” is a marketing segment, and it is the same question as the next slide. Chose a set of columns all at once, not ask-then-branch: no decision trees (DSCI 221).

Who Can Be Identified?

Six yes/no questions give \(2^6 = 64\) possible cards.

  • We have \(100\) students and only \(64\) possible cards. What does that guarantee?
  • In real data, a few “questions” with many possible answers are enough:
What identifies you Out of Identified
4 phone location pings (which tower, which hour) 1.5 million people 95%
4 credit-card purchases (which shop, which day) 1.1 million people 90%
about 8 movie ratings, with approximate dates Netflix subscribers 99%

Only 22 of the 100 are alone on their card; the class uses 54 of the 64 possible cards, and the biggest group shares one card four ways. Guaranteed: 100 students, 64 cards, so at least two share a card – in fact at most 64 can be alone, so at least 36 share (the pigeonhole principle; capacity from lecture 17). We come back to this dataset for the pigeonhole principle. Sources: de Montjoye et al., “Unique in the Crowd”, Scientific Reports 2013 (phone); de Montjoye et al., “Unique in the shopping mall”, Science 2015 (credit cards); Narayanan & Shmatikov 2008 (Netflix Prize). The classic is Sweeney (ZIP, birth date, sex) – left off the slide on purpose. The data-science response: k-anonymity – release data only if every row shares its profile with at least k - 1 others. Our class is not even 2-anonymous (22 students are unique). Why 4 pings beat 6 questions: a ping isn’t yes/no – it is one of thousands of towers times thousands of hours, millions of possible answers. Four of them give vastly more possible cards than people; we have 64 cards (6 bits) for 100 people. Fraction alone ~ e^(-n/m): e^(-100/64) ~ 21 students (actual 22). Bounds on \(|A\cap B|\)

Give upper and lower bounds on \(|A\cap B|\) for any \(A,B\subseteq U\).

 

\(\boxed{\phantom{\max(0,\ |A|+|B|-|U|)}} \leq |A\cap B| \leq \boxed{\phantom{\min(|A|,\ |B|)}}\)

 

Answer: max(0, |A| + |B| - |U|) <= |A n B| <= min(|A|, |B|). Upper: A n B is inside A and inside B, so it is no bigger than either. Lower: inclusion-exclusion rearranged, |A n B| = |A| + |B| - |A u B|, and |A u B| <= |U|. If |A| + |B| <= |U| the bound is 0. Our class: max(0, 56 + 45 - 100) = 1 <= |C n M| = 31 <= min(56, 45) = 45. From the counts alone, at least one student MUST drink coffee and be a morning person – the pigeonhole idea again, now for two sets. Privacy hook: published counts alone tell you things about individuals. Both bounds are achievable: push C and M apart (overlap 1) or put M inside C (overlap 45). Ask which arrangement gives each.

True Here, or True Everywhere?

The Empty Set (\(\varnothing\))

Definition: \(\varnothing\) (a.k.a. \(\{\}\)) is the unique set with no elements.

  • Cardinality: \(|\varnothing|=0\).
  • Universal subset fact: For any set \(A\), \(\varnothing\subseteq A\).
  • But membership is different: \(\varnothing\in A\) only if \(A\) literally contains the empty set as an element.

Examples

  • If \(S=\{a,b\}\): \(\varnothing\subseteq S\), but NOT \(\varnothing\in S\).
  • If \(T=\{\varnothing,\{a\}\}\): \(\varnothing\in T\).

Operations with \(\varnothing\)

For any set \(A\):

  • Union: \(A\cup\varnothing = \boxed{\phantom{|A|\,}}\)
  • Intersection: \(A\cap\varnothing = \boxed{\phantom{|A|\,}}\)
  • Difference: \(A - \varnothing = \boxed{\phantom{|A|\,}}\), \(\ \varnothing - A = \boxed{\phantom{|A|\,}}\)
  • Complement (in \(U\)): \(U - \varnothing = \boxed{\phantom{|A|\,}}\)
  • Cardinalities: \(|A\cup\varnothing| = \boxed{\phantom{|A|\,}}\), \(|A\cap\varnothing| = \boxed{\phantom{|A|\,}}\)

Answers: A; the empty set; A; the empty set; U; |A|; 0. Quick demo with a tiny set on the board or in code; emphasize “subset vs element.”

Equality?

Two sets are equal if they contain exactly the same elements. Think of a way to test set equality using set operations.

Are sets \(X\) and \(Y\) equal?

X = Y exactly when each is a subset of the other: (X & ~Y) and (Y & ~X) both select nobody. One line: ((X & ~Y) | (Y & ~X)).sum() == 0, or (X ^ Y).sum() == 0. Here they are equal (19 students each) – because M n I is a subset of C (previous slide). Two different-looking rules, the same set, in THIS class.

Identity

Experiment using the code below, and then complete the observation:

For any sets \(A\) and \(B\): \(\boxed{\phantom{(A\cup B)^c}} = \boxed{\phantom{A^c\cap B^c}}\)

(A u B)^c = A^c n B^c: De Morgan for sets. Equal for every pair of columns, and in every class – unlike Subsets and Equality?, which held only in ours. In code: (~(C | M) ^ (~C & ~M)).sum() == 0. 30 students are in neither C nor M. Code checks one dataset; proving it for every dataset is Friday (L21, DeMorgan for Sets).

Exit Ticket

  1. What does knowing which sets someone belongs to tell you about them?
  2. Code can check one dataset. How would you know something is true for every dataset?
  1. Each set someone is in is one answer about them, so knowing their sets is knowing their row. Enough sets together can single them out (Set Design, Who Can Be Identified?). And sets you know imply sets you don’t: in our class, a morning person who plays an instrument must drink coffee (M n I is a subset of C). Even counts alone force facts: |C| + |M| > |U| means someone is in both.
  2. Not by running more datasets – there are infinitely many. But one dataset CAN show a claim is false: add a tea-drinking morning violinist and “M n I is a subset of C” fails. To show a claim is always true, argue about one arbitrary student x: x is in (C u M)^c iff not (x in C or x in M) iff (x not in C) and (x not in M) iff x is in C^c n M^c – De Morgan’s law for propositions, one row at a time. Equivalently, a student can only be one of 4 kinds (in C or not, in M or not); check all 4 – that’s a truth table, and it covers every dataset at once. Friday’s proofs do this.

Fill in all eight regions. How many students are in none of the three?

Work from the middle out: centre 16; then the pairs minus the centre: C n M only 15, C n P only 13, M n P only 7; then each set minus its three inner regions: C only 12, M only 7, P only 17; total in C u M u P = 87, so none = 13. Each region is one combination of yes/no answers on three of the six questions – the Venn regions are the cards, restricted to three columns.

Element vs Subset

  • \(x\in S\) means \(x\) is one item in \(S\).
  • \(A\subseteq S\) means every element of \(A\) is in \(S\).

Examples (watch the braces!)

  • If \(S=\{a,b\}\) then:
    • \(a\in S\) is true; \(\{a\}\in S\) is false; \(\{a\}\subseteq S\) is true.
    • \(\emptyset \in S\) is false; \(\emptyset \subseteq S\) is true.
  • If \(T=\{\emptyset,\{a\}\}\) then:
    • \(\emptyset \in T\) is true; \(\{\emptyset\}\subseteq T\) is true.

Say aloud: “Curly braces change the type of thing. Elements vs sets of elements.”

Design a Set

Using only the six questions, write a mask that selects:

  1. exactly the person on your card;
  2. exactly your person and your neighbour’s;
  3. nobody at all.
  1. An AND of all six answers (or fewer, if fewer already pin them down). Some can’t be isolated: 78 of the 100 share their card with someone (Amara & Ravi, Leila & Hana, and many more).
