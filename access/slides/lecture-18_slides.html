Fixed Width

Goals

  • See what goes wrong when the number of bits is fixed: overflow.
  • Recognize overflow as arithmetic \(\bmod 2^b\).
  • Three puzzles to take away.

Technical Aside

  • int (pure Python): arbitrary precision (variable width)

  • NumPy / Pandas arrays: fixed-width datatypes (int8, int32, int64, uint64, …)

  • Signed range: \([-2^{b-1},\,2^{b-1}-1]\); Unsigned range: \([0,\,2^b-1]\).
  • Takeaway: the container chooses the encoding (width & signedness).

Greying a Pixel

A colour pixel is three numbers, (red, green, blue), each from \(0\) to \(255\), each stored as uint____.


To turn a pixel grey, set all three channels to their average: \[\text{grey} = \frac{r + g + b}{3}\]


What’s the grey for \((200, 100, 50)\)? \(\quad\) For white, \((255, 255, 255)\)?

Ask how many bits 0..255 needs: 256 values = 2^8, so 8 bits (capacity, from lecture 17). Fill in uint8: an unsigned 8-bit integer. 116 and 255. Easy – that’s the point. The next slide does it in numpy. (This is the CPSC 203 “make the poppies pop” computation.)

Greying in NumPy

Hidden setup (runs first): builds img, a 120 x 200 uint8 test image – red grows left to right, green grows top to bottom, blue 150, a white square in the middle – and show(before, after), which draws them side by side. Run it. The grey image should be a smooth wash and the white square should stay white. Instead: the white square is dark grey, and the smooth gradient breaks into diagonal bands. (The hidden setup turns off numpy’s “overflow encountered” warning, so the picture is the only clue.) 91% of the pixels come out wrong (checked locally).

What Went Wrong?

  • The white square was \((255, 255, 255)\). Why is it dark grey?

  • Why does a smooth gradient turn into bands?

  • Where are the edges of the bands?

White: 255 + 255 + 255 = 765, and a uint8 keeps 765 mod 256 = 253; 253 // 3 = 84. So white becomes (84, 84, 84). The bands: r + g + b rises smoothly across the picture, but each time it passes 256 (and again at 512) it wraps back to 0, so the grey drops to black and climbs again. The band edges are the lines r + g + b = 256 and 512 – diagonal, because red grows rightward and green grows downward. The picture IS mod 256. The fix, live: in grey, write r, g, b = int(p[0]), int(p[1]), int(p[2]). Python ints don’t overflow; rerun and the bands and the dark square are gone. That’s why the CPSC 203 poppies code converts each channel to int first. Subtle: numpy’s img.sum(axis=2) would have been right – sum quietly widens to a bigger type – but + between uint8 values does not.

Overflow

Consider addition, with 4 bits:

annotate 1101_2 + 110_2 and ask students to speculate on 1) how addition works, and how they would describe the overflow. (they’ll say it’s like mod 2^4) With \(b\) fixed bits, adding wraps \(\bmod 2^b\). 1101 + 0110 = 10011, and the 5th bit has nowhere to go: 13 + 6 = 19, and 19 mod 16 = 3 = 0011. Fixed-width arithmetic IS arithmetic mod 2^b – lecture 14, in hardware. That’s why uint8(255) + 1 is 0 in the demo. Back to the pixels: 200 + 100 + 50 = 350 = 1 0101 1110 in binary. A uint8 keeps the low 8 bits, 0101 1110 = 94 = 350 mod 256. White: 765 -> 253.


 


 

Punchline: Overflow occurs when the number you want to represent is too big to fit into the space you’ve reserved for it.

Signed Integers


\(b\) bits give \(2^b\) patterns, whatever we decide they mean.


Unsigned: the patterns mean \(0, 1, \dots, 2^b - 1\).


Signed: the same \(2^b\) patterns, half of them given to negative numbers. We won’t look at how, but we can count: \(b\) bits represent ____________.

Spend almost no time here. The point is information, not encoding: b bits can only ever name 2^b things, so giving some to negatives takes them away from positives. [-2^{b-1}, 2^{b-1} - 1]: still 2^b values (capacity), half of them negative, and 0 counts on the non-negative side, so one more negative than positive. If anyone asks how: a negative -x is stored as 2^b - x, which is -x mod 2^b, so addition just works mod 2^b. That’s why int8(127) + 1 is -128.

Puzzle 1: The Gold Chain

A traveller has a gold chain of \(7\) links. The innkeeper wants one link per night, paid each night, for \(7\) nights, and can give links back as change.


  • What is the fewest number of links you must cut?

  • With \(2\) cuts, how long a chain could you pay out this way? With \(k\) cuts?

  • What does this have to do with binary?

For you, if it comes up next class – not on the slide on purpose. 1 cut: cut link 3 (or 5), leaving pieces 1 (the cut link), 2 and 4. Pay the 1; pay the 2 and take back the 1; pay the 1; pay the 4 and take back 1 and 2; … Every amount 1..7 is a sum of distinct pieces of {1, 2, 4}: binary. 2 cuts: 23 links (cut links 4 and 11): pieces 1, 1, 3, 6, 12. k cuts: k single links, then pieces (k+1), 2(k+1), …, 2^k (k+1), for a chain of k + (k+1)(2^{k+1} - 1) = (k+1) 2^{k+1} - 1 (geometric sum, lecture 12). Brute force over every way to cut agrees: longest chains 1, 7, 23 for 0, 1, 2. Why the pieces double: if the pieces so far pay every amount 1..T, the next can be T + 1; the k singles cover 1..k, so k+1, then 2(k+1), 4(k+1), … Each piece is one more than everything before it, like 1, 2, 4, 8. (Puzzle from mathalon, Oct 9; planning/question-ideas.md.)

Puzzle 2: Nim

There are some piles of sticks: any number of piles, each with any number of sticks. On your turn, take at least one stick from any one pile. Whoever takes the last stick wins.

  • With piles \(1, 3, 5, 7\): should you go first, or let the computer go first?
  • Can you find a way to win every time you start from a good position? Try new piles too.

The playground: click a stick to take it and everything to its right. Modes: you first, computer first, two players; “New piles” for 2-5 random piles of 1-12 sticks. The strategy works for any number of piles of any sizes. The computer plays perfectly. On 1, 3, 5, 7, going first you will lose (tested: 20 of 20 random games). Let the computer go first and you can win. THE ANSWER (not for the slide): write the pile sizes in binary, one under the other. If every column has an even number of 1s (XOR of the piles is 0), the player to move loses against good play; otherwise there is a move that makes every column even, and you should make it. 1, 3, 5, 7 = 001, 011, 101, 111: columns have 2, 2, 4 ones – all even, so go second. Why it works (induction on the total number of sticks): from an all-even position, every move changes one pile, so some column becomes odd; from a position with an odd column, take from a pile with a 1 in the leftmost odd column to make all columns even; the empty position is all-even, so the player who always leaves all-even takes the last stick. Checked by brute force for every 3-pile game with piles up to 5. Example: 3, 4, 5 -> take 2 from the 3, leaving 1, 4, 5. Last Year at Marienbad (1961) is the film where one player never loses at 1, 3, 5, 7.

Puzzle 3: The Poisoned Wine

A king has \(1000\) bottles of wine for a party, and learns that exactly one is poisoned. The poison makes someone ill exactly one day after they taste it, even a drop, and the party is tomorrow.

He has servants who can taste the wine tonight. What is the fewest number of tasters he needs to find the poisoned bottle before the party?

Answer: 10. Number the bottles 0..999 in binary (10 bits, since 999 < 1024 = 2^10). Taster i tastes every bottle whose bit i is 1. Tomorrow, the set of ill tasters spells out the poisoned bottle’s number in binary. This IS the magic trick: each taster is a card, holding the numbers with a 1 in that bit; “which cards is your number on?” is “which tasters got ill?” Full circle to lecture 17. Fewer than 10 can’t work: 9 tasters give only 2^9 = 512 different outcomes, fewer than 1000 bottles (capacity again).
